EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
This 
executive 
summary 
concisely presents the findings 
and recommendations from the 
comprehensive climate finance 
development study in Moldova, 
commissioned by the Ministry 
of Economic Development and 
Digitalization in June 2024. The 
study aims to identify solutions 
to accelerate climate finance 
development, 
focusing 
on 
digitalization, 
climate 
technologies, and SME growth 
in 
the 
short 
term, 
while 
engaging larger corporations in the long term. The study is based on initial desk research and 
stakeholder consultations conducted during a mission in Moldova in June 2024. 
The study addresses the private sector's growing needs in Moldova amidst limited climate 
finance resources, access constraints, and capacity issues. These barriers hinder Moldova's 
alignment with international climate commitments, including the Paris Agreement. Addressing 
these needs requires adequate funding, capacity building, and technical assistance to ensure 
effective climate finance utilization through national institutions. 
 
Key Findings 
This executive summary outlines the detailed findings and recommendations for advancing 
climate finance in Moldova. Key recommendations are: 
⋅ 
Developing climate finance through additional fundraising via means of a new 
dedicated national climate fund, structured as a Fund-of-Funds (FoF). The 
Ministry of Economic Development and Digitalization in Moldova advocates establishing a 
dedicated national climate fund structured as a FoF to attract domestic and international 
financing. This report presents four scenarios for structuring the FoF: 1) utilize an existing 
institution like ODA; 2) support ODA's growth into a potential national Development 
Bank; 3) create separate funds managed by an external fund manager; 4) Establish an 
independent institution, such as a Development Bank. 
⋅ 
Developing climate finance via the accreditation of national entities. To advance 
access to international climate finance, Moldova – via its National Designated Authority - 
should promote 2-3 national entities for GCF accreditation. It is recommended that those 
entities be different to enable a broader outreach of climate areas to cover and utilize 
different financial instruments. For example, the accreditation of a governmental entity 
such as ODA could be key to attracting grant financing (subject to clearance of the 
accreditation thresholds). One commercial bank has already begun the accreditation 
process, while another commercial bank has shown concrete interest in doing so. 
⋅ 
Support national institutions’ capacity to develop pipelines of sustainable 
projects, reporting standards, and alignment with climate risk analysis and risk 
management practices. Strengthening the technical and fiduciary capacity of financial 
institutions in Moldova is crucial for supporting the development of climate finance. This 
can be achieved through bespoke technical assistance, which can help the institutions

understand sustainable finance, appraise green projects, and develop a stronger market 
for green products. Enhancing the technical knowledge of these institutions can also 
reduce the risk perception associated with green projects, encouraging banks to become 
more involved in sustainable finance. This report presents specific TA offerings that could 
be extended to FI and ODA to boost their technical capacity as well as organizational and 
fiduciary standards. 
⋅ 
Structure climate finance to provide concessional debt, anchor equity, 
portfolio guarantees and local currency lending. The most needed financial 
products appear to be concessional debt, with blending (co-financing) from donors such 
as GCF potentially being used to structure favorable loan conditions such as grace periods, 
longer tenors and subsidized interest rates. Portfolio guarantees are also critically 
important to reduce risk, particularly when collateral is insufficient, and loans are turned 
down. Local currency lending has also been discussed as an important mechanism to avoid 
the risks associated with currency fluctuations when borrowing occurs in hard currencies 
(typically EUR and USD). At the same time, the need for grants will remain, to cover 
education, awareness building, loan officers training, marketing and support to clients. 
Although it is still at the early stage of climate finance mobilization, there are readily 
identifiable pathways with strong indications of support and interest from public institutions 
and private businesses. A combination of short- to long-term programs will be necessary to 
unlock the supply of climate finance while also developing projects and businesses that meet 
stringent eligibility requirements to access this funding. 
 
Structuring of a FoF 
The establishment of a FoF structure has been promoted by the Ministry of Economic 
Development and Digitalization in Moldova, and it is regarded as an important solution to 
further the efforts of attracting domestic as well as international climate finance. The 
Government has allocated EUR 5 million to be utilized to support private sector SMEs via a 
VC/PE or impact fund vehicle. Different scenarios for the structuring of a fund of funds have 
been provided, including 1) working through an existing institution such as ODA; 2) 
supporting ODA through a natural progression and evolution to become a much stronger 
institution that could eventually morph into the national Development Bank; 3) a separate 
fund-or-funds that is managed by an external fund manager; 4) or a completely separate 
institution, such as a Development Bank itself, independent of any existing institutions. 
A FoF is an investment strategy that involves investing in a portfolio of other investment funds, 
rather than directly investing in individual projects. This approach provides diversification (for 
investors or governments) as it gives them exposure to a wide range of investment strategies 
and asset classes through a single investment. The growth in funds of funds can be attributed 
to an increasing number of both institutional and individual investors who seek access to 
hedge funds, impact funds and alternative investments but may not have the expertise or 
resources to invest directly in these complex strategies. In blending/ climate-related 
concessional finance, a FoF is becoming a popular choice for governments to pool funds and 
channel them towards activities that are linked to established programmatic priorities, such 
as for climate change adaptation and mitigation, sustainability investments, biodiversity, 
innovation etc. 
In Moldova, the discussion on the structuring of a FoF stems from the need to either support 
the strengthening of an existing institution (such as ODA) or building a new institution (such 
as a FoF that is managed by an outside fund manager; or a fully fledge development bank).

Developing climate finance through accreditation to the GCF 
For Moldova, there are opportunities to engage more deeply in international climate finance, 
including with the Green Climate Fund, the Adaptation Fund and the Global Environment 
Facility. 
 
With a focus on GCF, key advantages include: 
⋅ 
For national entities in Moldova, the potential to become accredited and access 
international climate finance through GCF can support national efforts to implement 
the targets of the Paris Agreement, strengthen financial institutions, and support 
sustainable economic growth based on climate technologies, innovation and 
digitalization. 
⋅ 
Moldova can develop multi-year sector-wide strategies for climate change programs 
as well as specific individual projects that more closely reflect the country’s priorities 
and needs, therefore enhancing country ownership over climate finance utilization. 
⋅ 
Once accredited, Moldova can receive support for project preparation as well as a flat 
fee as a percentage of the incoming grant or loan (upon negotiation, to a maximum 
ceiling of 8.5%). 
At the same time, there are drawbacks that need to be noted: 
⋅ 
The accreditation is a lengthy process (1 to 2 years) which requires the upfronting 
capacity to work on all the stages of the accreditation. 
⋅ 
It is an intrusive process, as GCF seeks to assess whether certain functions exist (e.g., 
financial control, procurement, project management, ethics and oversight, etc) and 
how they are implemented, and will request to obtain clear evidence of their 
implementation. 
 
Currently, Moldova does not have a nationally accredited entity (direct access entity). 
Moldova has received financing from the GCF via the EBRD for investments, and via FAO and 
UNDP for readiness programs. OTP Bank is the only institution that has begun the process 
of accreditation and is currently in stage 1. Two additional entities are proposed to seek 
accreditation: 1) ODA and 2) a commercial bank.

Blending and concessional instruments 
Consultations with stakeholders and financial institutions underscored the need to develop 
climate finance in Moldova to attract blended finance, promoting the deployment of various 
financial instruments. 
Concessional instruments are co-investments where donor contributions are allocated under 
favourable terms such as lower interest rates, longer grace periods, and tenors, often with a 
junior structure. This concessionality mitigates investment risks and improves the risk-reward 
profiles of climate investments that are not viable on commercial terms alone, expecting 
repayments for future investments. Common blended finance structures include debt, equity, 
risk-sharing, and guarantee products. Interest rate subsidies, funded by donor grants, reduce 
loan financing costs.

Guarantees 
Guarantees are capital-efficient risk minimization instruments in commercial and development 
banking, offering significant leverage. Banks can use their credit rating to reduce funding 
needed for a guarantee, covering any shortfall if called by the beneficiary, resulting in lower 
costs compared to loans. These are useful when financing costs are prohibitive or tenors are 
too short, addressing risks and barriers related to low credit risk profiles of borrowers. While 
less effective for liquidity shortfalls, guarantees work well in integrated lending and risk-sharing 
packages. Portfolio guarantees are preferable to individual project guarantees for faster 
decision-making and deployment. 
 
Local currency support 
Local currency finance is challenging in many developing countries due to high currency risk 
exposure. Local currency lending is vital for developing local capital markets, as it reduces 
vulnerability to foreign currency risks. Foreign currency debt impacts economic stability, 
capital flow volatility, and credit ratings in emerging economies. Multilateral funds like the GCF 
have provided concessional rates to facilitate currency swaps. While borrowing in USD or 
EUR has lower interest rates, it carries higher risks in case of local currency devaluation. 
Key advantages of local currency lending include that it: 
⋅ 
Mitigates the currency risks involved in foreign currency borrowing (where borrowers 
are more vulnerable to overheating, macro imbalances and systemic risks which 
prevent them from being able to pay the loan back) 
⋅ 
Addresses currency mismatches, as it matches the currency denomination of assets 
and liabilities, which enables companies to focus more on their core business rather 
than on managing exchange rate fluctuations 
⋅ 
Assists sectors that are crucial for sustainable development, such as infrastructure, 
housing and SMEs (who are particularly vulnerable to currency exchange rate 
fluctuations).

SECTION NO. 1 
BACKGROUND 
ACTIVITY CONTEXT 
Access to climate finance plays a crucial role in enabling countries to mitigate and adapt to 
the impacts of climate change. Moldova, as a small Eastern European country, faces numerous 
challenges in accessing climate finance. The bulk of climate finance that has flowed into 
Moldova is being managed predominantly through multilateral banks (such as the European 
Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD) or the European Investment Bank (EIB)), 
through regional programming (such as the Green Cities program, supported with co-
financing from the Green Climate Fund (GCF) and executed by EBRD), or through 
international climate finance provided via GCF, Global Environment Facility (GEF) or the 
Adaptation Fund (AF), and executed by international organizations, such as the United 
Nations Development Program (UNDP) or the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). 
 
At the same time, there is a significant need in Moldova to scale up the implementation of the 
national policy commitments and to enable both public and private institutions to commit to 
and align with recognized international standards for sustainability reporting and climate risk 
management. These needs include: 
 
⋅ 
Investing in renewable energy projects and technologies to reduce reliance on fossil 
fuels and mitigate greenhouse gas emissions. 
⋅

NOTES ON DEFINITIONS 
During the preparation and consultations for this assignment, several terms have been used 
to discuss the boundaries of coverage of funds that can be targeted at climate and 
environmental issues. Sustainable finance, green finance and climate are often used 
synonymously but there are some specific differences, as described below. 
 
The official definition by the United Nations Framework Convention for Climate Change 
(UNFCCC) states that ‘Climate finance refers to local, national or transnational 
financing drawn from public, private and alternative sources of financing — that 


 
12 
 
seeks to support mitigation and adaptation actions that will address climate 
change. It is needed for adaptation and mitigation’.6 
 
Climate finance includes mitigation finance, targeted at avoiding or reducing greenhouse 
gas (GHG) emissions or enhancing GHG sequestration; and at contributing to the stabilization 
of atmospheric GHG, in alignment with the Paris Agreement. It also includes adaptation 
finance, targeted at building the long-term resilience of people, livelihoods and places (natural 
and physical infrastructure) to demonstrable climate-related vulnerabilities. It is important to 
remember that climate finance is not the same as green finance. Green finance is an umbrella 
term to define the financing of investments that provide environmental benefits in the broader 
context of environmentally sustainable development. Subsets of green finance include climate 
finance, environmental finance and conservation finance. In parallel, the term sustainable 
finance is also widely in use today. Sustainable finance typically refers to the process of 
taking due account of environmental, social and governance (ESG) considerations in 
investment decisions. It refers to climate change mitigation and adaptation, as well as the 
environment (preservation of biodiversity, pollution prevention and circular economy). 
 
Furthermore, another point of interest is the promotion of innovation and climate 
technologies to support micro and small and medium-sized enterprises. For Moldova, in 
accordance with Law no. 179/2016 regarding small and medium-sized enterprises, article 5, 
the classification of enterprises is carried out based on the average annual number of 
employees, the annual turnover, or the total assets they hold, with the following categories 
established: 
a) Micro-enterprises - enterprises that have up to 9 employees, achieve an annual 
turnover of up to 18 million lei7 (USD1.02 million), or hold total assets of up to 18 
million lei; 
b) Small enterprises - enterprises that have up to 49 employees, achieve an annual 
turnover of up to 50 million lei (USD2.84 million), or hold total assets of up to 50 million 
lei; 
c) Medium-sized enterprises - enterprises that have up to 249 employees, achieve an 
annual turnover of up to 100 million lei (USD5.67 million), or hold total assets of up to 
100 million lei.

FUND OF FUNDS STRUCTURE 
A Fund-of-Funds (FoF) is an investment strategy that involves investing in a portfolio of other 
investment funds, rather than directly investing in individual projects. This approach provides 
diversification (for investors or governments) as it gives them exposure to a wide range of 
investment strategies and asset classes through a single investment. The growth in funds of 
funds can be attributed to an increasing number of both institutional and individual investors 
who seek access to hedge funds and alternative investments but may not have the expertise 
or resources to invest directly in these complex strategies. In blending/ climate-related 
concessional finance, a FoF is becoming a popular choice for governments to pool funds and 
channel them towards activities that are linked to established programmatic priorities, such 
as for climate change adaptation and mitigation, sustainability investments, biodiversity, 
innovation etc.

which has two windows and focuses on entrepreneurship in order to reach climate impacts

Box 1: Case Study 1 – Estonia’s Just Transition Fund 
The Just Transition Fund (JTF) in Estonia is a prime example of a fund of funds, a financial instrument 
designed to address the challenges associated with the transition to a sustainable and climate-neutral 
economy, as well as the needs to ensure energy security and resiliency against external (energy-related) 
shocks. It is a multifaceted instrument that aligns with the European Union's broader vision for climate-
neutrality by 2050. Established in 2021, the fund aims to support the country's transition away from 
carbon-intensive industries, ensuring that the process is socially and economically just for affected 
communities.8 This approach allows for a more strategic and coordinated allocation of funds, ensuring 
that the available resources are directed towards the most impactful and transformative initiatives. 
 
There are two windows in this fund: 1) "Economy and labor" with EUR 273 million; b) "Environment and 
social inclusion" with EUR 66.74 million. 
 
 
Box 2: Case Study 2 – Indonesia’s Environmental Fund 
The Indonesia Environmental Fund (IEF) – previously BPDLH - is a public service agency accountable to 
and structurally operationalized under the Ministry of Finance. It has a well-established structure under 
Indonesian law as a public service agency, through which it is considered a government entity that has 
the legal flexibility and autonomous authority to manage its operations, is not dependent on the state 
budget but can source funds from it. The IEF was established by the Government of Indonesia to mobilize 
funds from several sources (including multilateral and bilateral donors, development finance institutions 
and Indonesia’s own budget) to finance interventions (programs, projects) in the framework of 
preservation of environmental protection, climate change mitigation and adaptation, pollution prevention 
and other objectives that are closely linked to meeting the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). It 
has recently cleared additional regulations that enables IEF to structure itself as a fund of fund to enable 
multiple donors’ contributions and thematic windows. 
 
The mandate of the IEF is to carry out fund and project management functions covering five thematic 
windows: 
1) Agriculture, Forest, Land-use and Sustainable Ecosystems Management; 
2) Just and Affordable Clean Energy; 
3) Circular Economy and Zero Waste Emission; 
4) Health, Water and Food Security; 
5) Climate Adaptation and Disaster Risk. 
An additional window for biodiversity is also being discussed. 
 
A number of donors and institutions, such as the World Bank and the United Nations Development 
Program, have contributed resources to one single window. In order to enable multiple donors to 
contribute to the objectives of the IEF, a new ‘pooled source’ window is being designed. All the windows 
sit under an ‘umbrella program’ or fund, so that the IEF acts the Fund-of-Funds, and the thematic 
windows as sub-funds. Furthermore, the long-term strategy for the further capitalization of the fund is 
to enable carbon trading, with the revenue generated from the carbon sales being re-injected into the 
fund.

ACCREDITATION 
For Moldova, there are opportunities to engage more deeply in international climate finance, 
including with GCF, GEF, and AF. This section initially describes the accreditation process and 
then provides an analysis and recommendations on accreditation for key Moldovan 
institutions that have either expressed to become accredited or are considered to be strategic 
candidates, on the basis of the consultation with national stakeholders. 
Accreditation to the Green Climate Fund 
The “accreditation” to the Green Climate Fund is the formal, institutional-level process 
designed to assess whether the prospective entities are capable of strong financial and project 
management. The accreditation assesses policies and processes for safeguarding GCF-funded 
projects and programs and evaluates an entity’s ability to manage environmental and social 
risks that may arise at the project level. Working through accredited entities (which can be 
international/multilateral, regional or national) is the way in which GCF can deliver its mandate 
of supporting countries to adopt low-emitting and fully climate-resilient pathways to the 
United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). 
 
For national entities in Moldova, the potential to become accredited and access international 
climate finance through GCF can support national efforts to implement the targets of the Paris 
Agreement, strengthen financial institutions, and support sustainable economic growth based 
on climate technologies, innovation and digitalization. For example, through a value 
proposition centered around the principle of country ownership, Moldova may develop multi-
year sector-wide strategies for climate change programs as well as specific individual projects 
that more closely reflect the country’s priorities and needs. There are also drawbacks – the 
accreditation is a lengthy process – 1 to 2 years – which requires the upfronting capacity to 
work on all the stages of the accreditation, as illustrated below. This long timeline can be 
misaligned with the needs of the country, in particular the private sector, which tends to move 
swiftly. Capacity support to build strong pipelines is needed (as detailed in Table 8), targeting 
the financial institutions in the country (commercial banks, ODA as well as micro-finance 
providers), as well as SMEs. This, in turn, will facilitate the initial design of concept notes to 
be presented to the GCF as accreditation approaches the last stage. 
 
The costs associated with capacity improvements as well as the actual staff time spent on 
accreditation can be offset by the fact that entities received an “entity fee”, paid once as a flat 
rate and as a percentage of the incoming grant. The percentage reaches up to 8.5 per cent 
and can typically be used to cover expenses related to working with the GCF, including entity 
programming (i.e., providing GCF with tentative annual pipelines and strategic approaches) 
and program/project preparation.

Prospective organizations also find the GCF process quite intrusive, as GCF seeks to assess 
whether certain functions exist (e.g., financial control, procurement, project management, 
ethics and oversight, etc) and how they are implemented, and will request to obtain clear 
evidence of their implementation. The GCF accreditation process is evidence-driven, with the 
Secretariat requesting detailed proof of successful implementation of the types of 
projects/interventions for which the entity is applying. This presents a particular challenge for 
a newly established organization or fund, which may have not yet built a solid track record. 
GCF standards and requirements for accreditation encompass several aspects of policy setting 
and fiduciary standards. Where some processes or policies are missing, GCF will likely require 
an action plan by the applicant organization to move towards the development and then 
adoption of specific policies. The accreditation will likely take around 2 years, typically 
involving both external consultants and internal staff members. 
 
The following sections are to be developed during the accreditation: 
 
1. Background and contact information of the applicant entity 
2. Information on how the institution and its intended projects/programs will contribute 
to furthering the objectives of the Green Climate Fund 
3. Information on the scope of intended projects/programs and estimated contribution 
requested for an individual project or activity within a program 
4. Basic fiduciary criteria (accounting; financial management; audits; procurement policies 
and application; recourse and control mechanisms) 
5. Applicable specialized fiduciary criteria (project management; grant award) 
6. Environmental and social safeguards (ESS, up to medium risk) 
7. Gender. 
 
Furthermore, once an entity is accredited and begins developing projects and programs for 
GCF consideration, it is important to keep in mind that the process of completing the required 
project documentation and the evidentiary annexes will also require specific internal and 
external capacity, which can be estimated to cost around USD 200,000 per program/project. 
 
Currently, Moldova does not have a nationally accredited entity (direct access entity). 
Moldova has received financing from the GCF via the EBRD for investments, and via FAO and 
UNDP for readiness programs.

Accreditation to Other Funds 
The Adaptation Fund (AF) provides for accreditation under a national modality for national 
entities, a regional category for regional entities and then a third category for multilateral 
entities. Moldovan institutions can potentially apply under the national modality. The 
accreditation process for the AF is a ‘simplified’ version of the GCF one. There are ongoing 
discussions at the G20-Finance Ministers level on the integration of accreditation processes 
across climate funds, but no concrete decision has been taken yet. Once accreditation is 
achieved, programming can be structured to cover the following areas: 
⋅ 
Food security 
⋅ 
Supporting livelihoods, including infrastructure development, and a heavy focus on

⋅ 
Conservation and natural resource management; regenerative agriculture, 
agroforestry, silvo-pasture 
⋅ 
Disaster risk reduction and early warning systems 
⋅ 
Community-based adaptation. 
 
The Global Environment Facility (GEF) is another UNFCCC-mandate fund, which also 
supports a number of environmental conventions. The GEF does not operate a membership 
based on an accreditation (as GCF and AF have), but there is a screening process based on a 
decision by the Council. Currently, there is no opportunity for new entities to become 
accredited, in fact becoming an implementing entity of the GEF is traditionally challenging 
exactly because it is subject to a Council decision, i.e. political decision. However, national 
entities in Moldova can become executing entities of GEF’s implementing entities and support 
the execution of programmes and projects on the ground.

CONCLUSION 
Accessing climate finance is crucial to enable Moldova to mitigate and adapt to the impacts of 
climate change. Further developing the national climate finance ecosystem will require the 
involvement of various public and private institutions to develop a pipeline of high-quality, 
commercially viable sustainable projects. To achieve this, several areas of interventions have 
been identified in this study 
 
In the short term (months 1-12): 
⋅ 
Begin the process to operationalize a fund-of-fund structure within an existing 
institution, e.g. ODA through an extension of the current FACEM program. 
⋅ 
Draft coverage of capacity support/TA for ODA to cover the following areas: financial 
management, project management, ethics and fiduciary standards. 
⋅ 
Screen ODA for alignment with GCF-required policies, such as gender, the application 
of environmental and social safeguards, and anti-money laundering policies. 
⋅ 
Build a TA package of grant support for financial institutions to enhance their green 
product deployment, align with sustainability standards and consider climate risk in 
their operations. 
⋅ 
Build a TA package of grant support executed e.g. by Start-Up Moldova to help SMEs 
build green and sustainable projects. 
⋅ 
Approach GCF for accreditation of ODA plus 1 commercial bank (pre-screening 
stages). 
 
In the medium term (months 12-18): 
⋅ 
Continuation of the accreditation efforts for ODA and 1 commercial bank, guiding 
them through stages 1 and 2 of the accreditation process, including support to 
evidence all capacity requirements, in alignment with the accreditation process. 
⋅ 
Implementation of the TA package of grant support for financial institutions. 
⋅ 
Implementation of the TA package of grant support for SMEs for pipeline building. 
 
In the longer term (over 18 months): 
⋅ 
Provide advanced training for ODA (banking, finance). 
⋅ 
Finalise the accreditation process, approaching stage 3 (final negotiations and approval 
by the GCF Board. 
⋅ 
Once accreditation is achieved (or at a minimum, stage 2 is cleared and the entity has 
entered the legal negotiations in stage 3), support all new national accredited entities 
in the development of concept notes and funding proposals, to be submitted to the 
GCF. 
⋅ 
For an enhanced ODA/development bank, the process to obtain a credit rating can be 
started. Rating agencies will assess the portfolio and track record of the institution. A 
favourable rating can subsequently facilitate access to capital markets, including bond 
issuance.

ANNEX 3: NOTES FROM 
CONSULTATIONS 
This section provides a summary of key takeaways and conclusions from in-country 
stakeholder consultations. The list of consulted stakeholders is summarized in Annex 2. 
Financial Institutions 
OTP Bank 
OTP Bank is a private bank, part of Hungarian OTP Group (the Moldovan subsidiary was part 
of the Société Générale Group during 2007 – 2019 years), focused on retail, private, 
investment and corporate banking, and investment management. It offers individual, business, 
and legal entity loans. It has benefited from credit lines from multiple development finance 
institutions in the past and has worked amply in development finance, including with the 
European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD). As one of the oldest banks and 
the 4th largest in Moldova (share of assets), it was the first to launch a consumer loan and 
credit card. Furthermore, the bank is also involved in leasing and trade finance, as well as 
support for agricultural businesses, for which climate finance may prove useful. For instance, 
AgroFabrica supports agricultural businesses and provides loans for raw materials, land, 
irrigation, and equipment. 
 
Additionally, the bank has focused on gender and social inclusion programs, for example by 
launching Cherie Cherie, the first credit card exclusively for women. OTP Bank aims to 
support sustainable societal development, preserve cultural heritage, and enhance financial 
literacy. 
 
OTP Bank is the only institution in Moldova to have started accreditation to the Green 
Climate Fund. It is understood to be in Stage 1 (rapid gap analysis), and is preparing all the 
documentation, including new policies (such as the gender policy and granting policy) that are 
required to fulfil the requirements to clear Stage 1 evaluation. The accreditation is seen 
internally as a good opportunity to further bridge the gaps in climate and sustainable finance 
and mainstream ‘greening’ in OTP operations. 
 
Their approach and objectives in climate finance development. A key focus for the 
bank is on energy efficiency and renewable energy generation within the current product 
range. They mentioned that it is difficult to understand whether the market exists for other 
sectors/sub-sectors, such as sustainable agriculture. 
 
Financial products in agriculture: There have been some limited interventions for energy 
efficiency in agriculture. The lack of broader offerings in sectors such as sustainable agriculture 
also reflects the generally limited knowledge of climate change and the implications of climate 
risk. 
 
Concessionality. Blending can stimulate business, lower the cost of borrowing for clients 
and make a loan product more attractive. Local currency lending is also needed, to reduce 
risks associated with potential currency fluctuations when borrowing in hard currency. 
Guarantees are also needed, for example, to cover the potential exposure of banks when 
collaterals are missing. OTP is receiving guarantees via ODA but their deployment is 


 
56 
 
considered too slow because the guarantees are provided on a project-by-project basis rather 
than extended as portfolio guarantees. 
 
Gaps and Data: The bank mentioned support for training to enhance skills in other areas 
(non-energy) such as waste management, circularity and agriculture. Another area of 
development is capacity for data capture and reporting (through grant support). For example, 
it is difficult to provide data on the share of sustainable finance as a percentage of the total 
business volume of the bank because this is not tracked. The current work by the Central 
Bank on the sustainable finance roadmap (with IFC) and the future work on a green taxonomy 
can be useful in moving the issue forward. 
 
MAIB 
MAIB is Moldova’s largest commercial bank and lender with a market share of 34% in assets, 
37% in loans, and 30% of all profits earned by the Moldovan national banking system. It is a 
private bank that offers both individual, business, and legal entity loans. It has worked 
extensively with the EBRD in the last 3 decades and provides credit for micro-lending and 
SMEs. MAIB maintains one of the largest domestic branch network, with 53 branches and 50 
smaller outlets across all major industrial and rural areas. 
 
The sectors they are involved in include medicine, athletic ventures, education, and bringing 
equal opportunities to marginalized communities. Moreover, it takes a particular focus on 
agricultural security. As a part of the MAIB’s corporate social responsibility projects, it focuses 
on “planning good deeds for the environment.” In 2021, MAIB undertook projects to combat 
deforestation in Moldova. MAIB has aligned with the environmental and social standards 
required for EBRD borrowing. 
 
MAIB clients are mostly interested in energy efficiency (EE) for daily activities of MSMEs, 
e.g. manufacturing/production in factories; there have been many projects here in this area, 
typically on improving energy and cost savings through more efficient equipment with lower 
energy consumption. In the agro-industry: MAIB is the market leader for agricultural 
financing 50% market share in Moldova, 40% of MAIB portfolio. The bank also has investments 
in solar and wind power generation (approx. EUR 12 million in loan portfolio), including in 
some cases rooftops of agribusinesses. 
 
Resilience in agriculture and issues related to crops. In the past 5 years, 3 years were 
detrimental to agricultural development, for several reasons, some climate-related and some 
not. Firstly, there were failures in crop yields linked to increased high temperatures and heat 
waves. The second reason is related to the war in Ukraine. Typically, in a lower crop year, 
prices go up but with the war in Ukraine, the logistics of grain shipping changed, which also 
included a ‘dumping’ of Ukrainian crops on the Moldovan market, depressing prices. Many 
agribusinesses, supported through MAIB, began encountering problems with loan repayments, 
which led to many loan restructurings by the bank. There is also limited work in financing 
irrigation (no clear commercial market). There are no projects in energy storage, as mostly 
the energy is sold back to the grid (so energy storage is not thought of as a critical issue). 
 
Specific needs have been identified in supporting climate-resilience agricultural practices, 
water efficiency and irrigation. While irrigation is an issue, from a bank perspective, they do 
not see any request from clients, so they think the commercial angle is still unclear. Similarly, 


 
57 
 
in forestry, agroforestry and silvo-pasture, there are no investments in this area. The bank 
can potentially be interested as long as there is a commercial angle. 
 
Agricultural development and agri-tech. Moldova has great agricultural potential, but 
the bank thinks that currently there is too much focus on low-value crops. Crops are mainly 
exported, rather than processed in the country to create value in a specific product. The bank 
can support a shift from low value to high value products. There are some good examples 
in the country: ProGrain Organics is a very good company, with an equity investment fund 
from the Netherlands, they made a switch from normal to bio-crops. ProGrain in an export-
oriented company. They were not affected by the war. A second direction is to move from 
producing crops that have low rentability and high risk to more resilient activities such as 
cattle raising, growing vegetables, dry fruit and other produce. A third element is to invest 
more in the resilience of agriculture as well as food and water security. Additional work can 
be done in integrating energy in agricultural processes, e.g. PV in farms, and PV-pumped 
irrigation; biogas from manure; circularity and waste management; heat pumps (both rural and 
urban settings). 
 
Accreditation. MAIB is interested in principle but they are asking to understand the process 
and the required steps to initiate the accreditation. They tend to move very fast though (and 
there is some concern as to whether they will become frustrated by the process). They 
understand that accreditation can support concessional instruments, including local 
currency. Borrowing, for example through facilities such as the EBRD GEFF can be very 
expensive, as it is in Euro. Rates may be higher, resulting in clients not being able to afford 
those products, hence lower demand. 
 
Prime Capital 
Prime Capital is a non-banking, non-deposit-taking financial institution, IFRS compliant, with 
circa 1,500 clients and USD 21 million portfolio. It is a small institution with 55 employees 
and 4 branches. Lending is done from their own capital and through capital raising, loans from 
local banks; some grants for guarantee via USAID, and SIDA a line from EBRD. Prime Capital 
supports energy efficiency products; sustainable agriculture; guarantees for loans related to 
EE loans; loans for all categories (sizes) of borrowers: individual (up to 60% of the portfolio is 
individual loans, including mortgages; same rules as banks are applied), MSMEs as well as larger 
companies (but not huge corporates). They can consider financing/lending to start-ups but 
this will be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, based on the experience of the founder. Most 
investments are in MSMEs. Loans up to USD 1 million; median 30-50,000; the largest was 4 
million but this was an outlier. They also have micro-loans. E.g. solar, batteries, refrigerators 
etc. They have 2 projects in agriculture: biomass converting into biogas; pellet production 
from waste wood; transitioning from older to more energy-efficient equipment; and building 
eco-houses. Their sustainability efforts were guided by a policy, pushed by EBRD. As the 
company is young (and agile), they absorbed a lot from EBRD manuals and had a commitment 
to start with higher standards as well, including environmental and social safeguards (ESS) and 
prohibited practices. 
 
Prime Capital mentioned that they would benefit from more grant support for workshops 
and training for clients to enhance climate and sustainable activities. There is also ongoing 
support for semi-formal businesses to become formalized, e.g. guiding them through business 
registration and other required practices. 
 


 
58 
 
Gaps and needs: financing at low cost; technical knowledge. In some cases, longer tenors. 
Prime Capital may need to have more technical knowledge to structure products, more 
products and use more financial instruments, such as guarantees to cover collateral and 
minimize their risk, such as portfolio guarantees. For example, they currently accept 
equipment as a guarantee but that it is not very efficient. They would be happy to work with 
ODA under the proposed FoF structure if it moves fast, probably working on a 
portfolio/programming/facility basis. Additional support is also needed for market 
development, e.g. marketing and promotion to build awareness of sustainable opportunities 
from the clients. 
 
Needs: some concessional finance to lower the cost of borrowing; performance-based 
grants associated with energy efficiency measures and repayments track record; cover some 
incentives for example repayment of upfront fees or other fees paid by the borrower; Support 
to boost digitalization; monitoring and reporting on impacts. 
 
IFC 
Green finance at IFC in Moldova. IFC is working with the National Bank of Moldova to 
develop the regulatory framework for sustainable finance, including climate ESG and 
sustainability (e.g. also measuring climate risk to the financial sector). The Sustainable 
Finance Roadmap started in 2023, approved last week (June 2024). There are 4 pillars: 
awareness-raising training; 2) ESG; 3) Risk Management; 4) Green Taxonomy. IFC is seeking 
more assistance for the implementation of the recommendations made under the Sustainable 
Finance Roadmap. The next step is to work on the green taxonomy and the guidelines for 
sustainable loans (similar to bonds, having something that is the same for everyone). This work 
is closely linked with the National Standard Commission. The banks have also been consulted. 
For the taxonomy, the aim is to have the same definitions for all, IFC will adjust taxonomy to 
country; Moldova will seek to align with the EU taxonomy. Seeking to be adopted by the end 
of 2025. 
 
Discussion on ODA as the manager of the Fund-of-Funds. ODA as the fund manager 
is what the government wants, but for IFC, ODA’s capacity is low. ODA is managing a large 
number of programs already and the concern is that they will be taking on this work too 
slowly. For example, for the implementation (by ODA) of the guarantee fund provided by the 
World Bank, there are many institutional inefficiencies (like approving guarantees one by one, 
rather than on a portfolio basis). Financial management and reporting are not strong. Grant-
making is ok, but fiduciary/managerial issues still persist even after restructuring (by the WB). 
Recommending exploring other avenues as well, for example, separating asset/program 
management from financial management. 
 
EBRD 
EBRD head of office made some good recommendations on the mobilization of additional 
funds into the new fund-of-fund, in order to raise the image and standing of the fund itself 
(the current EUR 5 million provided by the Ministry of Economic Development will not suffice 
as anchor equity in the eyes of external investors). EBRD will be looking closely at a fund of 
a minimum EUR 25 million, with clarity in the exit of their investment.

Start-Up Moldova 
Start-up Moldova, with Olga Melniciuc. This is a non-profit, non-governmental 
institution, working mainly on supporting companies at the growth stage (less on initiation or 
incubation stages) and then for the scaling up of companies. They support companies with 
mentors and events (such as Start-Up Week, which took place in March). Their main 
objectives include working with community builders and start-up ecosystems, providing 
capacity building for start-ups; supporting hubs through mentorship. They can also support 
access to finance (planning to build own program/fund), to get local and international funding, 
matching external funding. They work on advocacy, e.g. drafting white papers for the 
government to support start-ups, and support governmental initiatives. They support 
companies with legal aid, e.g. GDPR compliance and intellectual property (IP). They also have 
cross-cutting activities including partnerships with foreign media. 
 
Their main funding source is USAID via FTA. Funding also comes from corporations linked to 
events or strategic planning (e.g. Premier Energy; MAIB etc). They have supported 250 start-
ups (their definition is a scalable business, generally young below 7 years, with model pivoting). 
 
Their main work is with technologies, which includes agri-tech; energy-tech; edu-tech; fin-
tech. Example: they have supported a new start-up in the energy sector to help with the 
optimization of solar batteries planning, production and consumption for networks (to avoid 
overload); drones & building platforms for those who have drones to support data capture; 
‘Green’o’ to collect data on CO2 emissions – they raised EUR 2 million. 
Needs: 
⋅ 
Capacity – building full tech understanding; access to funding is always an issue; 
⋅ 
More is needed (training but also events where forums where people get skills as well 
as contacts and networking). 
⋅ 
Access to foreign markets (leading to relocation in some cases, which is ok for scaling 
as long as the companies maintain the registration and main office in Moldova. 
⋅ 
No knowledge on climate generally, many founders come from the in spaces, less 
experts in the field of energy or climate. 
 
Targets: Growth of all start-ups by 30% yearly; increase the survival rates (now 2%, which is 
quite low). 
Screening: At the moment, they are not very selective. However, if the company is very 
young or has an idea with no demonstrable revenue, then it is a no. Funding decisions are 
made on demonstrable commitment and investment from the owners, the presence of 
experts in the company, the dynamics of the team; the presence of some seniority in the 
team; some proof of traction in the market; and a final due diligence on the company. 
Thresholds of fundraising span from EUR 700,000 to 2 million. 
 
Discussion on ODA to manage FoF. Positive, they could be good as asset managers but 
capacity will need to be raised internally, as they tend to be slow. For them, an advantage 
point of ODA is the mandate and the fact that they can cover cross-cutting issues (including 
digitalization and tech as enablers of green finance).

Ministry of Energy

Net zero 
⋅

Improving the performance of heating/cooling (e.g. introducing meters in every

Concessional Instruments 
Concessional instruments are structured as co-investments, where the concessional donor 
contributions are allocated with favorable conditions, such as interest rates that are lower 
than market rates; longer grace periods and tenors; and often with a junior (or subordinated) 
structure. In this way, concessionality is used to mitigate specific investment risks and help 
rebalance risk-reward profiles of climate investments that are unable to proceed on strictly 
commercial terms. There is an expectation of reflows (repayments) from the client for future 
investments or other uses. While blended finance can be structured in a variety of ways, the 
most common instrument structuring is debt, equity, risk-sharing, or guarantee products. 
 
A fundamental element of concessionality is blended finance, which is a structuring approach 
that allows organizations with different objectives to invest alongside each other while 
achieving their own objectives (whether financial return, social impact, or a blend of both). 
The main investment barriers for private investors addressed by blended finance are (i) high 
perceived and real risk and (ii) poor returns for the risk relative to comparable investments.

Guarantees 
Guarantees can be capital-efficient instruments in commercial and development banking and 
as such can generate significant leverage, since banks can use their credit rating to reduce the 
amount of funding provided for a guarantee, on the understanding that they will cover any 
shortfall if the guarantee is called by the beneficiary. This capital efficiency benefits the parties 
by leading to lower costs than a loan of the same face value. These arrangements can also be 
used when finance providers are willing to provide finance in principle, but the cost of doing 
so may be prohibitive or the tenors are too short.

Local Currency Support 
The development of local currency finance remains a challenge in many developing countries 
due to the high exposure to currency risks. Local currency lending is critical to support local 
capital markets development, as it boosts resilience by alleviating countries’ vulnerability to 
foreign currency risks. The level of foreign currency debt affects the stability of economic 
growth, the volatility of capital flows, and the credit ratings of emerging economies. In some 
countries, multilateral funds such as the GCF have provided concessionality (in the form of 
subsidized interest rates) that has been used to pay for currency swaps. From a client’s 
perspective, borrowing in USD or EUR (or in any other major currency) bears lower interest 
rates than borrowing in local currency, but entails a higher risk in case of currency 
devaluations. 
 
Key advantages of local currency lending include that it: 
⋅ 
Mitigates the currency risks involved in foreign currency borrowing (where borrowers 
are more vulnerable to overheating, macro imbalances and systemic risks which 
prevent them from being able to pay the loan back) 
⋅ 
Addresses currency mismatches, as it matches the currency denomination of assets 
and liabilities, which enables companies to focus more on their core business rather 
than on managing exchange rate fluctuations 
⋅ 
Assists sectors that are crucial for sustainable development, such as infrastructure, 
housing and SMEs (who are particularly vulnerable to currency exchange rate 
fluctuations) 
⋅ 
Develops local capital markets (especially in terms of resilience), which have been 
linked to economic growth.
