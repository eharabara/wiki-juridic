Tackling informal employment Increasing incentives for labour force participation Macroeconomic stability (20 mins) Briefly summarise the key structural issues related to macroeconomic stability in Moldova (large current account deficit, trends in remittances, trends in export diversification, public debt, demographics). The current account of the balance of payments of the Republic of Moldova constantly reflects a deficit, which decreased by 20.5% in 2023, after significant increases, in 2022 – by 46.1% and in 2021 – by 91.6%, being influenced by the geopolitical situation in the region and post-pandemic decline of the economy, respectively. As a ratio to GDP, the current account deficit of the Republic of Moldova is one of the highest (-11.9% in 2023) among the neighbouring countries and in the region. The evolution of the current account of the Republic of Moldova shows a stable and direct dependence by foreign trade in goods negative balance, which is partially offset by the positive services, primary and secondary income balances. In 2023, the external trade in goods deficit amounted to USD 4 887.80 million and decreased by 5.9% compared to 2022. More than a half of total exports are agri-food products, followed by machinery, appliances, equipment. In the last two years, reexports of mineral products, mainly gasoline and diesel, oriented to Ukraine, increased substantially. The main categories of imported goods are: mineral products, machinery, appliances and equipment, products of chemical industry, plastics, rubber and articles thereof etc. In the last years there was a clear reduction in both exports and import to/from CIS countries and an increase in the external trade in goods with EU countries. Over the years, EU consolidated its leading position in imports to the Republic of Moldova. Romania is constantly the main trading partner of the Republic of Moldova and holds about a half of total exports to and imports from EU. In 2023, the surplus in external trade in services amounted to 885.82 million USD, decreasing by 2.5% compared to the previous year, due to the more significant increase in imports (+183.03 million USD), than the increase in exports (+160.43 million USD). The main driver of the increase in services imports was the travel services, while the exports were impacted by IT services increase. It`s important to mention that export of IT services practically tripled compared to 2019. The primary income surplus increased by 3.5 times compared to 2022, up to USD 209.81 million, due to a 21.8% increase in inflows, up to USD 1 094.04 million, while outflows increased by 5.5%, to USD 884.23 million. The growth in inflows was determined by 4.0 times (up to USD 179.35 million) increase in income from reserve assets management, and by a 6.7% (up to USD 889.75 million) increase in compensation of resident employees for work performed in favour of non-residents. Outflows were driven by a 2.1-times increase (up to USD 138.68 million) in interest payable on other investments. Starting with 2019, outflow of income on equity and investment fund share increased by 2.6 times. Secondary income registered a surplus of USD 1 818.52 million, by 4.4% more than in 2022. Personal transfers received from abroad by residents decreased by 6.7% and amounted to USD 1 122.61 million, while their outflows decreased by 19.0% and amounted to USD 334.27 million, negatively influencing the surplus of secondary income. The value of technical assistance, humanitarian aid and grants received within international cooperation by all institutional sectors increased by 2.2 times and amounted to USD 575.92 million. Personal remittances received by residents of the Republic of Moldova during the 2023 year amounted to USD 1 946.29 million (-2.0% compared to 2022) and accounted for 11.8% relative to GDP. Personal remittances received by Moldova`s residents from non-residents are more than 4.0 times higher than outflow to non-residents. More than a half (57.9% in 2023) of received remittances are from EU, from the other countries – 29% and CIS – 13.0%. The Republic of Moldova registered one of the highest values of personal remittances relative to GDP in the region (11.8%). At the end of 2023, the position of foreign direct investment liabilities (FDI) increased by 11.9% and accounted for 39.1% of the country`s external liabilities. Approximately 60% of FDI liabilities position are represented by equity and investment fund shares, of which, around 85% are from EU investors. Financial and insurance activities, wholesale and retail trade, repair of motor vehicles and manufacturing industry hold the biggest shares in the position of liabilities in the form of direct investment (equity). The Republic of Moldova accounts one of the lowest shares of inward FDI position relative to GDP (for 2022 - 29.8%). The inflow of FDI in 2023 relative to GDP amounted to 2.5%. Gross external debt increased constantly during the last 5 years, on an average Y/Y rate of 9.6% and at the end of 2023 amounted to USD 10 465.7 million. Public external debt accounts around 1/3 of gross external debt, being represented mainly by long-term liabilities (99.9%). Private external debt held roughly 65% of total, of which the long-term debt accounted to 54.3% and short-term – 45.7%. The Republic of Moldova maintains a sustainable external debt. The gross external debt to GDP ratio in 2023 is 1.7 times lower than EU ratio (63.3% versus 109.6%). At the end of 2023, the position of reserve assets increased by 21.9% compared to the end of 2022. The evolution of reserve assets by components shows the significant increase of position of securities to around 79% of the total position as of the end of the 2023. Official reserve assets position meets all the sufficiency criteria. In the last five years, Moldova's central government debt has increased due to a series of overlapping crises. As of 2023, the country's general government debt stands at 35.3% of GDP, primarily driven by central government debt, which constituted 98.3% of total. By December 31, 2023, central government debt increased slightly to 34.6%. The projections indicate a moderate increase in general government debt, estimated at 37.0% in 2024 and 38.3% in 2027. These levels, however, remain within sustainable limits, reflecting our prudent fiscal management practices. For the purpose of state debt management, the Ministry of Finance periodically elaborates a medium-term Government Debt Management Program (Strategy). The latest program for the years 2024-2026 was approved by Government Decision No. 855/2023 . The program sets target ranges for the main risk and sustainability parameters, which are monitored monthly. The Executive Board of the National Bank of Moldova (NBM), by its Decision No. 101 of 19 May 2022, approved the Regulation on responsible consumer lending by banks , in force as of 1 July 2022, as part of the package of recommended macroprudential measures to achieve the intermediate objective of preventing and/or limiting excessive credit and debt growth. The regulation introduced requirements on household borrowers-based measures: loan-to-value ratio, debt service-to-income ratio and maximum maturity limit. The loan-to-value (LTV) ratio applies to residential real estate new loans to households and cannot exceed 80%, and if the loan is partially and/or fully offset and/or guaranteed by the state (e.g., under First Home Program) or is partially guaranteed by bank deposits – the LTV limit is calculated considering the offset and/or guaranteed credit share (to be deducted). At the same time, the debt service-to-income (DSTI) ratio applies to household new loans and should not exceed 40% of the borrower's confirmed net income. As an exemption, the DSTI cap of 55% is applied if the borrowers’ average monthly net income exceeds at least twice the size of the average monthly nominal salary per economy approved by the Government for the year in which the credit decision is taken. Also, to achieve the goal pursued by the introduction of DSTI, the maximum maturity of the loan granted to the household was limited to 5 years for consumer loans and to 30 years for loans for real estate investments. The similar requirements are applied for non-bank credit organizations by Regulation No. 20/5/2022 on responsible lending requirements for non-bank credit organizations, in force as of 1 September 2022. Now, the NBM has started the process of amending these two regulations on responsible lending by requesting opinions, proposals and recommendations of the market participants and other stakeholders on the current versions of regulations. CAPITAL MARKET The Moldovan capital market is not making sufficient contribution to the financing, risk management and investment needs of the real economy. Although established in the early 1990s, the capital market in Moldova could be characterized as a market in formation, which still feels the negative consequences of the mass privatization process. Capital market participants hold less than one percent of total financial sector assets. Both the number of listed companies and the amount of securities traded on Moldova Stock Exchange is quite small. There are only 37 companies listed on the Moldova Stock Exchange, most of them represent the financial sector (banking, insurance). No state owned enterprises (SOEs) are present on the stock market. CAPITAL MARKET PARTICIPANTS: MSE - The Moldova Stock Exchange was established in 1995 and is the country’s only Stock Exchange. The transactions on the MSE are executed via the Automated Integrated Trading System. The MSE trading system is outdated and lacks capacity and functionality, resulting in low efficiency of trading. The current, possible solution is to facilitate integration of stock market in regional markets, by bringing expertise and technology, aiming to promote local capital market development. CSD - Single Central Securities Depository (it was established in 2018), an institution that performs registration, accounting and settlement of corporate securities, state securities and NBM instruments. Because, CSD performs activities for all type of securities, supervision of CSD is split between NBM and NCFM. The lack of interconnections with other CSDs discourages cross-border trading. Interconnections between CSDs are essential to support the settlement of cross-border trades and to enable market participants to access larger, foreign markets. There is an intention to have a standard linkage with Romanian CSD in order to facilitate access to Romanian Stock Exchange (BVB) of local issuers. Intermediary system – Investment firms. Banks holding in-house license for investment activities and brokerage firms are the main actors in distribution of all types of securities for client. However, only 3 from 7 banks are predominant on capital market. The brokerage companies were created in the process of privatization and are too small to have the capacity to actively participate in the adequate process of creating value for the capital market (liquidity provider, investment adviser, preparing analysis). Hence, they usually provide issuers with strategies for market exit. Following the harmonization of legal framework, the market created opportunities for development of new business in the capital market, including authorisation of the first investment advisor in local and foreign instruments and submission of the request for authorization of the voluntary pension fund establishment. In addition, two companies requested authorization for crowdfunding services. There were no IPOs for shares in recent years on the stock exchange; all initial and additional share issuances took place among shareholders (a cumulative volume of EUR 1.879 billion of issued shares during the period 1992-2003). However, starting in 2023, for the first time, Moldova's capital market was marked by the admission of government securities (with a maturity period of more than one year) for trading on the stock exchange, as well as a commercial bank carried out the first public offering program of corporate bonds (4 issuances) with a total value of EUR 20 million. In the Republic of Moldova there are various tools to assess labour market competencies and needs for policy development. 1. Short-Term Labor Market Forecast (1 year): The National Employment Agency annually develops the Short-Term Labor Market Forecast and the Occupation Barometer to anticipate labor market requirements and the skills demanded by employers. The 2024 Labor Market Forecast and Occupation Barometer involve several stages, including an annual survey of approximately 3000 economic agents, followed by data analysis and weighting, and the presentation, promotion, and dissemination of the Forecast and Barometer. The short-term forecast also includes a module applied every three years to collect information on skills demanded in the labour market, as reported by employers. 2. Medium-Term Labor Market Forecast (3 years): This forecast aims to anticipate future labor market trends, including labor demand and supply, as well as skills needs. This process is crucial for strategic planning and developing effective labor and education policies. Recently the Independent Analytical Center "Expert- Grup " developed the labor market forecast for 2023–2026 under the "EU4Moldova" program. The forecast is based on two scenarios regarding the economic evolution of the Republic of Moldova between 2023 and 2026. According to the forecast, employment indicators are expected to improve due to positive economic developments. The employment rate is projected to increase from 40.5% in 2022 to 44.8% (according to the first scenario) or 43.7% (according to the second scenario) by 2026. 3. Development of Occupational Standards: The process of development of occupational standards is coordinated by the Ministry of Education and Research and Ministry of Labor and Social Protection that provide methodological support. These standards identify professional competencies and align initial and continuous professional training with labor market requirements, ensuring the quality of workforce training. A special role in the development of occupational standards is held by Sectoral Committees, that ensure alignment between professional training and the needs of the labour market. The activity and responsibilities of Sectoral Committees are regulated by the Law on Sectoral Committees for Professional Training. Occupational standards reflect the main roles and responsibilities in a field and provide a detailed description of the tasks, knowledge, and skills necessary for effective job performance. To date, 89 occupational standards have been developed and approved. 4. Profiling of Districts in the Republic of Moldova: District profiles are created at the district level by the Territorial Employment Subdivisions to reflect the labor market demand and supply situation at the district level. These profiles are used for planning employment policies. In 2023, profiles were developed for all 35 districts of the Republic of Moldova. 5. Labor market analyses: The Labor Market Observatory, as a part of the National Employment Agency, develops comprehensive labor market analyses and informs the education system about market needs and requirements. Its main activities involve: identifying skills gaps, skills mismatches and future needed skills; providing structural changes within the workforce per sectors of the economy; informing state institutions, companies, the unemployed and educational institutions. It uses short-term questionnaires with various modules and data analysis using barometers. 6. In the context of the war in Ukraine, the European Commission proposed in July 2022 to amend the 2021 TEN-T proposal to integrate sections of the transport networks from Ukraine and Moldova to improve connectivity. Significantly, a corridor linking Romania to Ukraine will be included in the TEN-T network, specifically the Baltic Sea-Black Sea-Aegean Sea corridor. Moldova's railways and roads are designated as part of the "extended" TEN-T network, aligned with this corridor, serving as vital transport links, promoting connections with the EU, and facilitating trade and other economic activities. The development of these corridors will be supported through various financial mechanisms, including the Connecting Europe Facility. At the same time, the country is crossed by three major rail transport corridors. The Northern Corridor connects Moldova with Ukraine through several border points, serving the cities of Bălți and Ungheni, with Ungheni being the busiest rail border point with Romania. The Central Corridor provides the connection between Ungheni and the capital Chișinău, extending to Ukraine through the Odessa region, passing through Transnistria. The Southern Corridor connects the central network with the Odessa region through the Basarabeasca station and has been rehabilitated up to the Berezino station to avoid Transnistria. Given Moldova's strategic role as a transit country between Ukraine and the EU and its potential to join the EU, aligning the railway gauge with the European one is essential to avoid transshipment or axle changes at the Romanian border. Several initiatives are underway to develop the railway sector. In 2012, CFM began modernising its fleet by acquiring 5 refurbished DMU units, and in 2016, an EIB-EBRD loan and an EU grant facilitated the purchase of 12 diesel locomotives delivered in 2020. Repair works progressed on the Bender- Căușeni - Basarabeasca-Etulia-Giurgiulești railway section, including the repair of 40 km of track, replacement of 38 turnouts and 13,000 sleepers, and cleaning of the railway bridges at the Căușeni station. The energy crisis, accelerated inflation, the war in Ukraine, the complicated external environment, and severe drought negatively influenced the national economy’s development in 2022-2023. As a result, GDP decreased by 4.6% in 2022, according to data published by the National Bureau of Statistics. The economy struggled to recover the following year due to ongoing crises, registering an anemic growth of only 0.7% in 2023 (according to preliminary data). These multiple crises have also led to structural changes in the economy: the share of services in GDP has increased, while the share of the productive sectors has decreased. The most significant negative contribution to GDP dynamics in 2022 was generated by the agricultural sector, which saw a 23.5% decrease in gross value added (GVA). This decline was driven by reductions in both crop production (maize, wheat, sunflower, fruits, nuts and berries, potatoes) and animal output. Consequently, the share of agriculture in GDP declined to 8.1%, down from an average of around 11% over the past decade. In 2023, although GVA in agriculture increased by 32%, the sector's share of GDP further decreased to 7.6%. Strongly influenced by the rise in raw material and energy prices and the disruption of supply chains, the GVA of the industrial sector decreased by about 18% in 2022-2023. Manufacturing, the most affected branch, experienced a 15% decline due to the consequences of the war, producing the highest negative impact on the sector. The industries with the most significant decreases include automotive, furniture production, metal construction, and fabricated metal products. The crisis also impacted other industrial sub-sectors: the energy sector contracted by 38% due to rising energy prices, and the mining industry shrank in the context of reduced construction activity. Overall, the industrial sector reduced GDP growth by 1.4 p.p. In 2023, the downturn in the industrial sector continued, with GVA decreasing by 12% in manufacturing and mining, while the energy industry grew by 11%. Consequently, the share of the industrial sector in GDP decreased from 12.7% in 2021 to 11.2% in 2023, including a drop in the manufacturing share from 9.6% to 8.2%.   Demand for construction decreased due to major uncertainty caused by the war in Ukraine, rising prices for construction materials (+19.1% in 2022), and budget constraints. As a result, the GVA generated by construction activity in 2022 decreased by 9.7%, reducing GDP growth by 0.8 p.p. In 2023, the construction sector continued its recession, with GVA falling by around 14%. Consequently, the share of this sector in GDP fell to 6.2% in 2023, down from an average of 8.4% over the past six years. Meanwhile, the services sector expanded by 3.2% in 2022-2023, particularly in the information and communication, accommodation and food service, financial and insurance, education, real estate, and health and social work sectors. Much of this growth can be attributed to the influx of Ukrainian refugees and the subsequent increase in consumer demand. As a result, the share of services in GDP increased from 54.6% in 2021 to 61.1% in 2023.
