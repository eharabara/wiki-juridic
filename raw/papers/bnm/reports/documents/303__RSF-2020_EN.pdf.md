SUMMARY
During 2020, the financial system of the 
Republic of Moldova showed resilience, the 
economic and social conditions influenced 
by the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic 
being characterized by uneven 
developments and materializations of risks 
in the segments of the economy of the 
Republic of Moldova. 
Moldova's economy has been hit by the 
pandemic that caused a global recession 
due to the cessation of economic activity in 
order to slow the spread of the virus. 
Under these conditions, in addition to the 
internal lockdown, the recession was also 
influenced by multiple external factors, 
such as economic contraction in the EU and 
main CIS partners of the Republic of 
Moldova, as well as limiting the movement 
of people and goods, thus reducing both 
imports, as well as exports. Against the 
background of the governmental measures 
undertaken, the budget deficit has 
increased and the state debt has increased. 
Despite the challenges for the period under 
review, the banking sector continued to be 
characterized by a satisfactory financial 
performance, maintaining strong positions 
of profitability and solvency. 
In the unfavorable economic context and 
the restrictions related to the COVID-19 
pandemic, the credit portfolio of the 
banking sector has maintained its growth 
rate. The non-performing loans ratio in 
total loans decreased, largely due to the 
increase in the total portfolio. Credit risk 
continued to pose the most significant 
threat to the stability of the banking sector. 
At the same time, the credit risk analysis 
highlights the effectiveness of prudent risk 
management policies implemented by 
banks, which have managed to maintain 
sufficient capital positions in 
unprecedented crisis conditions to absorb 
estimated potential losses even in the most 
pessimistic asset quality scenarios. 
Although interest rates charged by banks 
showed uneven developments, the bank 
interest margin increased. Banks' open 
foreign exchange positions were within 
prudential limits, and the impact of the 
sector's direct exposure to exchange rate 
volatility was reduced. The liquidity of the 
banking sector remained significantly 
higher than the regulated requirements. 
Under the restrictions imposed during the 
year, banks were able to maintain the 
current liquidity at the prepandemic level, 
including further minimizing the risk of 
maturity and currency mismatch between 
assets and bonds. 
The non-bank lending sector moderated its 
expansion on all subcomponents. The 
balance sheet and the aggregate loan 
portfolio volume of the non-bank lending 
sector remained practically at the same 
level as at the end of the previous year, and 
the most significant was the profitability of 
NCO, which halved compared to the profit 
accumulated in 2019 (including the 
creation of additional provisions in 
accordance with the prudential 
requirements in force in 2020). The risk of 
over-indebtedness of the population and 
the risk related to foreign currency-linked 
loans remain the main risks associated 
with the non-bank lending sector. 
Measures have been taken to strengthen 
the supervisory and regulatory framework 
for this financial segment. 
Improving national legislation is essential 
to promote a secure, stable and transparent 
financial sector. In 2020, changes were 
made to the legislative and regulatory 
framework in the context of consolidating 
financial stability, continuing to reform 
the financial system by aligning with best 
practices and international standards in 
the field, but also improving 
infrastructure.

 ECONOMIC AND REAL CONTEXT 
1.1. 
Economic developments 
During 2020, the economy of the Republic of 
Moldova was hit by a pandemic that caused a 
global recession due to the cessation of economic 
activity in order to slow the spread of the virus. 
Thus, in addition to the internal lockdown, the 
recession was also influenced by multiple 
external factors, such as the economic 
contracting in the EU (-6.1 percent) and the main 
partners in the CIS (-3.0 percent in Russia), also 
restricting the movement of persons and goods, 
and as a result, both exports of goods and 
services decreased by 11.5 percent and imports - 
by 10.5 percent. To partially offset the impact of 
the pandemic on the real economy, a number of 
measures have been taken at government level 
that have led to a widening of the budget deficit 
and an increase in government debt, which in 
2020 accounted for about 30 percent of GDP. 
The negative dynamics of the GDP of the EU 
member states, which registered a decrease of 
6.1 percent of the GDP, of the CIS countries (-
3.0 percent), but also of the advanced 
countries as a whole (-4.7 percent) contributed 
negatively to the economic situation of the 
country.  
The slow global vaccination process, the 
maintenance of tensions between the US and 
China, as well as the decrease in international 
trade have had a slowing effect on the 
economic recovery.

The economic downturn that began in the 
second quarter was mainly caused by the 
sectors related to wholesale and retail trade, 
maintenance and repair of motor vehicles and 
motorcycles, transport and storage, 
accommodation and catering activities that 
were affected firstly by the lockdown imposed 
by the authorities. Net taxes on products also 
fell due to the slowdown in economic activity 
and measures to support the economy. Other 
sectors were affected as well, such as the 
extractive industry, manufacturing, 
education, professional and scientific 
activities, but to a lesser extent. The 
"information and communications", "financial 
and insurance activities" and "construction" 
sectors remained the least affected. The 
economic recovery in the fourth quarter of 
the year was mainly generated by the 
“construction”, “public administration and 
defense” sectors; compulsory social insurance; 
education; health and social assistance ”,“ 
financial activities and insurance ”, as well as 
the associated tax collections.  
In 2020, the disposable income of the 
population experienced a favorable dynamic, 
reaching the level of MDL 3,096.6 per month 
per person, increasing by 7.5 percent 
compared to the previous year. The growth 
rate of disposable income was lower than that 
recorded in 2019 of 20.9 pp, still being positive, 
despite the effects of the pandemic. The 
structure of the main components of 
disposable income remained broadly constant, 
with income from employment continuing to 
be the predominant component.

State debt risks

cost of state debt of the Republic of Moldova 
identified as market risks are:  
1) Currency risk; 
2) Refinancing risk; 
3) Interest rate risk. 
Currency risk is one of the main categories of 
risks associated with the government debt 
portfolio, affecting both its cost and volume. 
At the end of the reference year, 56.9 percent 
of the government debt portfolio belongs to 
the external government debt (Figure 1.2.5), 
fully denominated in foreign currency, the 
share of foreign government debt increasing 
by 1.4 pp.

At the end of 2020, the National Bank 
published for the first time the residential 
real estate price index (RPPI index). This 
index is a measure of the evolution of the 
offer price for residential real estate in 
Chisinau, on a quarterly basis.  
In order to compile the RPPI index, the NBM 
collects data on real estate sales offers on 
broad selling platforms. The index is divided 
into 2 substrates related to the primary and 
secondary market, weighted based on the 
data of the Land Registry Department of the 
Public Services Agency.  
The hedonic model based on characteristics is 
used to compile the RPPI index. Based on that 
model, the evolution of the price of the 
typical real estate is obtained, defined as the 
average of the characteristics of all real estate 
identified on the market.

related to the primary real estate market 
registered a more pronounced increase, 
compared to the one related to the secondary 
market, the values of the RPPI index at the

percent respectively.

Box 3. Measures taken in the context of the 
COVID-19 pandemic with an impact on 
financial stability 
In the difficult crisis caused by the COVID-19 
pandemic, the NBM approved measures to 
support individuals consuming bank loans, as 
well as offering flexibility to banks in 
approaching businesses that have bank loans. 
Thus, in March 2020, the Executive Board of 
the National Bank issued decisions allowing 
the licensed banks flexible management of 
payment obligations of individuals in 
difficulty of payment for loans contracted 
(until 31.07.2020), as well as the postponement 
or modification of the due dates of the 
payments to the credits granted to the 
economic agents (until 30.06.2020), without 
classifying them in a harsher category than 
the one existing at the date of the decisions. 
At the same time, the NBM allowed banks to 
use temporarily, until a later date, their own 
funds maintained to meet the capital  
conservation buffer, banks should continue to 
pursue appropriate policies for the 
recognition and coverage of non-performing 
exposures and to conduct sound capital and 
liquidity planning as well as robust risk 
management. 
At the same time, in order to ensure that the 
licensed banks maintain an adequate level of 
own funds and liquidity to ensure their 
viability and stability in the event of 
uncertainty regarding the further evolution of 
the COVID-19 pandemic, in April 2020 the 
NBM recommended banks to refrain from 
distributing dividends to shareholders and 
other forms of capital distribution until 
30.09.2020, and subsequently, given that the 
risks and uncertainties generated by the 
pandemic spread continued during 2020, in 
December 2020 the NBM recommended that 
banks refrain from dividend distributions 
until the audit companies present the audited 
financial statements for the end of 2020.

2.6. 
Risk related to information and 
communication technologies 
Over time, the banking sector has witnessed 
many significant transformations due to 
advances in technology, changes in the business 
model, changing regulatory standards and many 
other internal and external factors. Banks have 
become much more advanced, and the security 
aspect has been greatly improved. However, 
banking operations have become much more 
complicated 21.  
As the economy and financial system become 
more digitalized, cyber risk increases more 
and more. During the COVID-19 pandemic, 
financial institutions have been at the 
forefront of responding to cyber risk. Their 
already high exposure to cyber risk has been 
further exacerbated by the shift to remote 
work and other operational challenges22. 
According to the risk map published by the 
European Central Bank, ICT deficiencies and 
cybercrime are among the top three risks 
facing the EU banking sector23. 
Following the assessment of ICT risks in 
licensed banks in the Republic of Moldova in 
2020, the most exposed areas are: data 
availability, business continuity and 
outsourcing of certain services. Also, during 
2020, there was an increase in the number of 
“social engineering” attacks on the clients of 
licensed banks, aiming at stealing money from 
bank cards. 
Although operations and business continuity 
have come under pressure, banks have so far 
largely managed to limit the impact of the 
COVID-19 crisis on critical operations and 
functions, while remaining largely 
unaffected. At the same time, no major 
downtime incidents have been reported that 
can be attributed to the crisis.

4. MACROPRUDENTIAL INSTRUMENTS AND MEASURES 
4.1. 
The macro-prudential instruments of 
the NBM 
In the domestic financial sector, the level of 
systemic financial risk is considered to be 
relatively low. This is due to the slow recovery of 
lending activity. During the year, all banks 
continued to comply with capital buffer 
requirements. 
Capital conservation buffer is designed to 
ensure that banks build capital buffers out of 
times of stress, reserves that can be used to 
cover losses that may arise from banking. The 
requirement is based on simple capital 
conservation rules designed to avoid breaches 
of the minimum capital requirements. 
The implementation of the capital buffer at 
the level of the domestic banking sector was 
established by the Regulation on capital 
buffers of banks, the rate of capital buffer was 
set at 2.5 percent of the total amount of 
exposure at the risk of banks. 
Following the crisis induced by the Covid-19 
pandemic, the NBM adopted a decision 
allowing banks to temporarily use (until a 
later date) the capital buffer previously 
established, maintained capital can be used to 
absorb any deterioration in the quality of the 
credit portfolio and to support the financing 
of the business environment and the 
population. In this context, banks were 
encouraged to continue to promote 
appropriate policies on the recognition and 
coverage of non-performing exposures and to 
carry out sound capital and liquidity planning 
as well as robust risk management. The 
measure is in line with international good 
practice and is similar to the decisions

Box 7. New liquidity requirements for 
banks (LCR) and limiting the risk of over-
indebtedness (leverage) 
Regulation on liquidity requirements for 
banks (approved by DEB of the NBM no. 
44/2020), as well as the Leverage regulation 
for banks (approved by DEB of the NBM no. 
274/2020) is a component part of the package 
of regulations developed by the NBM within 
the TWINNING project on strengthening the 
capacity of the NBM in the field of banking 
regulation and supervision in the context of 
the transposition of Directive 2013/36 / EU 
and Regulation 575/2013, implementing the 
Basel III international regulatory framework 
in the EU. 
Liquidity regulation is one of the main 
reforms of the Basel Committee to ensure the 
sustainability of the banking sector: liquidity 
coverage indicator. This indicator was 
introduced following the financial crisis of 
2007, which demonstrated the importance of 
liquidity for the proper functioning of 
financial markets and the banking sector. 
LCR's objective is to promote the short-term 
resilience of banks' liquidity risk profile. This 
is done by ensuring that banks have an 
adequate stock of high value liquid assets, 
which can be easily and immediately 
converted into private cash markets to meet 
their liquidity needs in crisis situations over a 
period of 30 calendar days. The 
implementation of the LCR indicator will 
contribute to improving the capacity of the 
banking sector to absorb the shocks 
generated by financial and economic stress, 
regardless of the source, thus reducing the 
risk of their spread from the financial sector 
to the real economy.  
The approved regulation helps to promote 
the short-term resilience of the bank's 
liquidity risk profile by ensuring that it has 
sufficient liquid assets to survive  
a crisis over a period of 30 calendar days. 
The approved regulation sets the value of the 
liquidity coverage indicator at at least 100 
percent, with the phased implementation of 
the indicator ranging from 60 percent (as of 
October 1, 2020) to 100 percent (as of January 1, 
2023). 
Leverage regulation is one of the reforms of 
the Basel Committee to limit the risk of 
excessive indebtedness of the banking sector 
during periods of economic growth and 
significant decline in banking assets in the 
downward phases of the business cycle. This 
indicator was introduced following the 2007 
financial crisis. Risk-based own funds 
requirements (own funds ratio) are essential 
to ensure sufficient own funds to cover 
unexpected losses. However, the crisis has 
shown that these requirements alone are not 
enough to prevent banks from taking 
excessive leverage risks.  
Thus, the leverage indicator, which is 
calculated by dividing the capital 
measurement indicator (Tier 1 equity) by the 
bank's total exposure measurement indicator, 
has been introduced and is expressed as a 
percentage. The Basel regulations of 15.12.2019 
set the minimum level of the leverage effect 
indicator (3 percent). According to the 
European legal framework, the quarterly 
observation of the leverage effect took place 
during the period January 2014 - June 2020. 
From July 2021 the minimum level of the 
leverage effect indicator in the European 
Union is 3 percent. 
The above-mentioned regulation stipulates 
that banks will report to the NBM the 
information related to the leverage effect 
indicator, and the NBM following the 
observation and analysis of the reported data 
will establish the minimum level of this 
indicator. A 3-year observation period of the 
leverage indicator is expected (from 30.03.2021 
to 31.12.2023), during which the NBM, taking 
over the EU practice, will monitor the leverage 
indicator, its components and its behavior in 
relation to the risk-based requirement.

on 30.04.2020, the NBM approved the

payment of contributions to the BRF.  
The provisions of the regulation are based on 
the practice of EU states as a reference model, 
but also take into account the specifics of the 
national banking system. The main aspects of 
the regulation concern: (i) the financing 
mechanisms of the BRF; (ii) a description of 
the process for calculating and paying the 
contributions; (iii) description of the 
methodology for calculating contributions. 
The BRF consists of annual contributions (in 
order to reach the target level set by law), 
extraordinary contributions (if the annual 
contributions are insufficient in the opinion 
of the resolution authority), loans and other 
forms of support. At the same time, the 
calculation of the total annual contributions 
for the entire banking system is mainly based

- 
number of years remaining until the

-
