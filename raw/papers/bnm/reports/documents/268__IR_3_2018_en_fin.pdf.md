Summary
The new medium-term inﬂation forecast (eight quarters) was prepared based on of the updated
macroeconomic analysis and underlying assumptions, taking into account domestic and international
economic developments, including the dynamics of population consumption, remittances, foreign
exchange market indicators, stock indices of commodities and energy resources, as well as changes in
the external and internal trade conditions.
The annual inﬂation rate will follow a downward trajectory over the next three quarters and fall below
the lower limit of the inﬂation variability interval, reaching the minimum level of 1.9% in the fourth
quarter of 2018. Later, the trajectory will reverse, inﬂation returning within its variability interval in
the second quarter of 2019 and recording 6.5% in the third quarter of 2019. Core inﬂation will record
a similar trajectory, largely inﬂuenced by supply factors. The growth rate of food prices will decrease
in 2018, reversing to the upward trend in the second half of the next year. The annual growth rate of
regulated prices will fall to -4.4% in the current quarter, but will accelerate afterwards, exceeding a 10%
level in the second half of 2019. Having recorded an increase during this quarter, the annual growth
rate of fuel prices will have a downward trajectory throughout the forecasting period.
Aggregate demand will remain moderate over the forecast horizon as a result of the restrictive monetary
and ﬁscal conditions to be maintained throughout next year.
The existing trade and geopolitical factors determine global economy to grow at a moderate pace.
Currently, the US trade protectionist actions, which were replicated by China and other relevant
economies, generate the major risk to global economy. The complexity of this risk arises from the
uncertainty of the extent and viability of the phenomenon. Considerable risks are also generated by
more restrictive ﬁnancial conditions promoted in advanced economies, affecting emerging economies.
Until recently, the negative effect of the transfer of ﬁnancial capital from emerging to advanced
economies was offset mainly by higher international commodity prices. However, the stabilisation of
energy prices associated with trade tensions has led to slowing down of the growth rate of international
prices, which could negatively affect economic growth in emerging economies.
The Eurozone’s GDP projection was revised downwards based on a lower growth rate recorded in the
ﬁrst quarter 2018 due to low temperatures and political tensions, which affected Italy and Germany. The
economy of the Russian Federation achieved some stability, overcoming the immediate consequences
of international economic sanctions and beneﬁting from high oil prices. The trajectory of the EUR/USD
cross-rate forecast has been updated as the US dollar has appreciated in recent months. The oil price
will remain in the range of 70-80 US dollar/barrel over the next period and will record an average level
of 72.2 US dollar/barrel in 2018 and 73.0 US dollar/barrel in 2019. Trade tensions between the US, China
and the European Union have provoked a recent drop in international food prices, pushing down their
trajectory for about two quarters.
The current inﬂation forecast was revised downwards through the second quarter of the next year and
upwards for the rest of the forecast horizon. This revision was driven by an upward revision of core
inﬂation through the ﬁrst quarter of the next year and a downward revision for the rest of the forecast
period. The growth rate forecast for food prices was revised downwards throughout the forecast period.
The growth rate forecast for regulated prices was revised downwards through the second quarter of the
next year and upwards for the rest of the forecast horizon. At the same time, the growth rate forecast
for fuel prices was revised upwards throughout the forecast period.
The annual CPI fell from 4.7% in March 2018 to 3.2% in June 2018, thus falling below the inﬂation target
interval. The CPI subcomponents kept recording a steady decrease in the core inﬂation level, driven
by modest aggregate demand and the appreciation of the Moldovan leu, which were slightly offset by
occasional sectoral shocks. Following a high base period of the previous year and a good crop of fresh
fruits and vegetables in the second quarter of 2018, the annual food price index recorded a steady
decline over the reference period. The annual rate of regulated prices continued to decline, driven

by the high base effect. The rise in international oil prices coupled with a slight depreciation of the
domestic currency against the US dollar determined a rise in fuel prices in the domestic market. In the
second quarter of 2018, the actual value of the annual CPI growth rate recorded no deviations from
the level projected in the previous forecasting round. Over the next period, the annual inﬂation rate
will remain below the lower limit of the inﬂation interval following the cuts implemented in electricity
tariffs as well as a negligible impact continually generated by aggregate demand on prices. At the same
time, the inﬂation’s downward trend could be also attributed to the secondary effects of a decrease in
tariffs on natural gas and electricity. During the second quarter of 2018, the annual industrial production
price index maintained its downward trajectory, thus determining a number of CPI subcomponents to
maintain the same trajectory over the next period.
The positive evolution of the national economy in the ﬁrst quarter of 2018, as was projected in the
previous forecasting rounds, was favoured both by the growth of aggregate demand as well as by the
impact of a rich crop recorded in the previous year that fuelled the rise in exports. However, the growth
rate recorded lower values, with GDP increasing by 3.7% compared to the ﬁrst quarter of 2017. The
growth in gross ﬁxed capital formation was supported by investments in both the construction and the
machinery and equipment sectors. The stock change generated a positive impact on the dynamics of
economic activity. As in the previous year, in the ﬁrst half of this year the volume of exports continued
to grow, mainly supported by good agricultural crop and the demand from the EU countries. The volume
of imports has also increased against the background of an increasing domestic demand as well as the
appreciation of the domestic currency. By resource categories, in the ﬁrst quarter of 2018, the GDP
growth was largely determined by positive contributions generated by the industrial sector, trade, the
information and communications sector, as well as by net product taxes. Statistical data for April-May
2018 outline conditions for a continued economic growth in the second quarter of 2018. Estimates
suggest that economic growth will maintain in the second half of the year.
In the ﬁrst quarter of 2018, the number of the employed population remained at the level of the beginning
of the previous year, while the number of unemployed population diminished, which determined a
negative growth rate of the economically active population. The unemployment rate has decreased
both compared to the beginning of the previous year and to the fourth quarter of 2017.
In April-May 2018, the annual growth rate of the state budget’s revenues recorded positive evolutions,
rising by 14.1% compared to the similar period of 2017. However, the state budget’s expenditures
recorded a similar trend, increasing by 14.6%, compared to the similar period of 2017. This trajectory
could generate pressures that would push upwards the domestic demand in the coming years. At the
same time, the market situation favoured a further decrease of interest rates on state securities, which
during the second quarter of 2018 decreased on all types of maturities.
In the second quarter of 2018, the excess liquidity amounted to 9.2 billion lei. A high amount of excess
liquidity was largely generated by monetary issues following the NBM’s interventions in the forex market
and by public sector spending. Under these circumstances, banks preferred placements in low-risk
instruments, such as placements in state securities or the National Bank Certiﬁcates (NBC).
Monetary aggregates in the second quarter of 2018 recorded a positive but moderate dynamics as
compared to the previous quarter. At the same time, the main contribution to this evolution was largely
determined by the increase in the volume of sight deposits, both in national currency and in foreign
currency.
Following the trend of the base rate applied to the main monetary policy operations of the National
Bank of Moldova and as a result of the liquidity surplus registered in the banking system, the average
interest rates of banks licensed both on credits and on deposits continued their downward trend.

In recent months, the US dollar has appreciated against other
international currencies, the USD index reaching the level of 94.35
(Chart 1.10) at the end of June 2018. The Federal Reserve System
(the Fed) increased the interest rate range by 0.25 p.p. at its

In addition, at the US Congress meeting in
July 2018, Jerome Powell, the head of the Fed, conﬁrmed his
intention to increase the interest rate range over the course of this
year. Investors also believe that the intensiﬁcation of trade-related
tensions between the US and its trading partners allowed investors
who trade in US dollars to have the highest returns on investment,
compared to transactions in other G10 currencies.
The EUR/USD cross rate decreased signiﬁcantly in the second
quarter of 2018. On a quarterly average, the euro depreciated
3.0% against the US dollar (Chart 1.11). The depreciation can be
attributed to a technical correction as well as to the impact of an
economic slowing down and political tensions periodically rising in
the Eurozone. However, in June 2018, the annual inﬂation rate in
the Eurozone reached 2.0% (the ECB-targeted level), whereas the
unemployment rate fell to 8.5% in May 2018. These developments
led the ECB, at its June 2018 meeting, to lay down a plan of the
programme on ceasing asset purchases. Thus, the ECB intends
to procure assets worth 30 billion euros per month through the
end of September 2018, after which, in October-December 2018,
the asset purchases will be reduced to 15 bln. euro per month.
Starting 2019, the ECB will no longer purchase assets, but the
base rate will remain at zero level through the summer of 2019.

1.4
Risks and uncertainties
External sector
• Trade-related
and
geopolitical
tensions.
Trade
protectionist actions continued in the second quarter of 2018.
A major decision that was made by the United States and
China was to introduce, as of 6 July 2018, bilateral taxes on
imports of goods worth 34 billion US dollars. Moreover, there
is a lot of talk about the US intention to introduce taxes for
car imports from Europe. In addition, tensions were raised by
the US decision to withdraw from the nuclear agreement
signed with Iran, which implies stopping of Iranian oil
purchases by the US.
The problem is further scaled up by the US pressures exerted
on its partners to join the decision on cutting Iranian oil
imports, as well as by Iran’s threats of closing the Strait
of Hormuz in the event of international blockade. All these
decisions and actions create far-reaching risks of disrupting
international trade, affecting international commodity prices
and manipulating foreign exchange parities (disinﬂationary).
• More
restrictive
ﬁnancial
conditions
in
advanced
economies.
At its meeting held on June 14, 2018, the
European Central Bank published a plan on cutting total
asset purchases by the end of 2018 and maintaining the
base rate at 0.0% until the summer of 2019. Since in June
2018 the annual inﬂation rate in the Eurozone has already
reached the target level of 2% set by the ECB, experts
anticipate that, starting the summer of 2019, the ECB will
increase the base rate. At the same time, the Fed, the Bank
of Japan, the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England
continue to tighten monetary policies, thus causing migration
of ﬁnancial capital from the emerging economies to the
advanced ones. This phenomenon affects foreign exchange
parities and emerging economies (pro-inﬂationary).
