# 05. Knowledge map: the LLM Wiki

Status: active. Last revised 2026-09-21.

This document records what the legal knowledge layer actually contains, so that answers can be honest about their own foundations. It is the reference behind the confidence line required by document 03.

## Why this document was wrong, and what changed because of it

The version of 4 September said seven codes were "outside coverage entirely": procedură civilă, executare, vamal, muncii, contravențional, penal, procedură penală. All seven were already ingested and anchored the following day. The document was wrong within a day of being written, and wrong in the direction that wastes the base: it understated coverage, so an answer would have been marked not anchored when the text was present, anchored and verifiable.

The cause is the one the vault already solved for itself. The vault's `CLAUDE.md` carries a coverage table generated from the files by `_meta/coverage/build_coverage.py`, precisely because a hand-written description drifts within hours when more than one agent writes to the folder. This document restated those counts by hand and inherited the drift.

So the rule changes here. **This document no longer restates what the generated table holds.** It names where the table lives and records only what a script cannot produce: judgement, failure modes, and priority. For the current inventory, read the coverage table in the vault's `CLAUDE.md`, or run the script.

The same error repeated between 6 and 10 September, in the same direction. The version of 6 September listed the Constitution, Constitutional Court decisions, CNPF secondary legislation and Law 149/2012 as absent. All four entered the base in the four days that followed, while this document still said they were missing. The lesson is now structural: this document is checked against `git log` at every session close, not only when someone remembers it.

That rule has a blind spot the two prior errors didn't expose, and the 17 September revision existed because of it. The document sat untouched from 10 to 17 September while three sessions and 40 commits changed the base: the acquis findings layer was unfrozen and reverified in full, the in-force register's blindness to point-structured acts was fixed, a first extract of the Association Agreement entered the base, and nine more acts were ingested. The "check against git log" rule was written for the wiki's own session close, which can flag that this document is stale but cannot rewrite it, because the master lives in the claude.ai project and the copy in the vault may not be hand-edited. Flagging drift and correcting it are two different steps, and only the first one runs automatically. Worse, one specific claim did not just age, it became false: the entry describing the in-force register as blind to point-structured acts, with `HG-743-2024` as the example, was true on 10 September and wrong from 16 September, when that defect was fixed. It survived a week past its own correction because the document restated a CLAUDE.md open question in its own words instead of pointing to it, the same mistake the coverage-table rule above was written to prevent, applied to a different kind of content. The update rule below was extended to cover it.

This document was rewritten on 17 September and checked again two days later, on 19 September, at Eugen's request and before any new mistake was suspected. It was already behind, but in a different way than the errors above: nothing in the 17 September text had become false. One further session had closed the citation graph's own audit of its unresolved references, answered the Civil Code's oldest named gap, and ingested the single most-cited act the base did not yet hold, and none of that made an existing sentence wrong the way the point-structured-acts case did on 16 September. It only left the document silent about work that changes two of the four entries below, in "The four ways an answer can still be wrong". A false sentence must be caught before anyone relies on it; an incomplete one only wastes the chance to say more. This revision answers the second kind of gap, not a repeat of the first, and the working tree was checked for uncommitted changes before this was written, not only the commit log; see the update rule below.

## What the wiki is

A structured knowledge base of Moldovan legislation, built to be queried by a language model rather than read by a person. It lives as an Obsidian vault at `C:\Users\harab\wiki`, under git since 5 September with a private remote on GitHub, governed by its own `SCHEMA.md` and `CLAUDE.md`.

Its original perimeter was the legislation supervised by the National Financial Market Commission (CNPF). That perimeter has been passed several times over. The base now carries the Constitution, the Civil Code and all the other codes, company law, the Fiscal Code, the Administrative Code, the domestic ring of acts a stagiar needs for corporate and administrative work, the banking perimeter in Romanian, the rules of the Depozitarul central unic (DCU, the central securities depository), CNPF subordinate acts ingested case by case, and a declared policy perimeter. Since 15 September it also carries a first extract of the Association Agreement, the domestic civil and commercial litigation ring (arbitration, mediation), the Electoral Code, and a growing set of acts that entered not because a plan named them but because the corpus itself, read mechanically, showed they were cited and missing.

Since 5 September the vault holds two declared perimeters, `legal` and `policy`, distinguished by a frontmatter field and by different citation rules in `SCHEMA.md`.

## The layer structure

- **Raw layer.** The source text of each act, ingested from legis.md (or, for DCU and some regulator acts, from the issuer's site), anchored at article level where the act has articles. Acts structured in points carry no anchors and are cited by point.
- **Structured layer.** The pages listed in `index.md`, split between the two perimeters, under `entities/`, `concepts/`, `comparisons/` and `queries/`. `queries/` is empty since the EMIR drafting pages were archived. `index.md` holds the count. It is not repeated here.
- **Findings layer.** The analytical conclusions drawn on top: mandate boundaries, transposition gaps, acquis alignment. Frozen and labelled 6 September, reverified and unfrozen 16 September, see below.
- **Control layer.** The machinery in `_meta/` that lets the other three be checked rather than trusted: the validator, the anchoring verification chain, the coverage generator, the in-force register, the register of provisions annulled by the Constitutional Court since 8 September, and the citation graph of acts held and cited since 10 September.

## The findings layer: frozen 6 September, unfrozen 16 September

The previous version measured the asymmetry: the Moldovan perimeter laws as full text against EU acts as extracts of roughly 170 lines each, with MiFID II at about 170 lines against 2,611 lines of Law 171/2012. Two consequences followed. A transposition finding compares a full Moldovan text against a summary of the EU text, so a gap classified as "not transposed" may be a gap in the extract. And the pages recording the comparison were last updated in July, before the raw layer was rebuilt.

The response chosen on 6 September was a freeze, not a re-verification, because with EU full texts deferred a re-verification could not be completed. Executed 6 September: the 17 `acquis-*` concept pages and the transposition matrix carried `confidence: low`, the tag `unverified`, and a one-paragraph notice; the label is declared in the schema spec so the validator knows it. No finding was re-argued.

The freeze was lifted on 16 September, once each page could be checked against something firmer than a summary: the Moldovan text already anchored, and, where it mattered, the EU text read directly on EUR-Lex rather than from the 170-line extract. ## The four ways an answer can still be wrong

They fail with decreasing loudness. This is the part of the map worth reading twice.

**One, the provision is not in the base.** Still the most common, and this document is what makes it knowable. The correction at the top shows the error runs in both directions, and in more than one form: not just a coverage table restated by hand, but a limitation's status restated by hand, or a real gap left unnamed until someone asks the right question, as the general-practice perimeter above was. An area recorded as uncovered when it is covered, or a defect recorded as live when it is fixed, is as costly as the reverse, because it downgrades an answer that was in fact anchored, or warns against a fault that no longer exists.

**Two, the provision is in the base but the text is old.** The generated table flags the acts whose consolidation is more than two years old. The systematic test was run on 6 September: every act with a doc_id was checked against the legis.md version history, and the report is at `_meta/coverage/currency-sweep-2026-09-06.md`. Three acts were behind and were refreshed the same evening; one was abrogated on legis.md and is now recorded as such. What the sweep taught: legis.md publishes consolidations dated in the future, to 2030 and one "at the date of EU accession", so the newest version is not the version in force today, and an act counts as behind only when a newer version with a past date exists. The sweep is a report, never a refresh, because a refresh drops the amendment markers of earlier consolidations (see the known gaps section). The "Publicat" rule of the coverage script was checked in the same pass and hides nothing.

Confirmed a third time on 19 September, in a case that would have cost seven whole articles: legis.md's own document identifier does not track version recency. The PDF held for `COD-325-2022` carried a doc_id that looked plausible and a text that looked complete, 245 anchored articles, but the version list showed it three consolidations behind the current one, which has 252 articles including an entire regime the PDF's date predates. A higher doc_id is not reliably a later date; the only safe check is the version list itself, never the number. See "Present since the 17 September version" above for how that doubt was resolved.

**Three, the provision is in the base, current, anchored and hash-verified, and still is not the law today.** The register at `_meta/inforce/in-force-register.md` carries the current count in its own header line. Document 03 section 4 makes consulting the register mandatory before citing.

One limit of the register was closed 16 September and is not restated here: CLAUDE.md's open questions record it. One remains live: where a deferred amendment rewrites an article instead of repealing it, the version in force today is absent from the file altogether. No marker can supply a text that is not there, and the answer is not anchored whatever the anchor says.

A second, different limit was found on 16 and 18 September: the register reads only the bracket marker left in the text of an amended act, but a deferred entry into force can instead be written in prose in the amending law's own transitional article, with no marker anywhere in the act being amended. `L-9-2026`, a brand-new mediation law, defers two of its own provisions this way; `L-66-2017` does the same to two Civil Code articles it introduces (today's arts. 64 and 96), harmlessly, since that deadline passed in 2019. The register cannot see either case. Anyone dating a provision introduced by an amending law should read that law's own final article, not only the bracket markers in the text it amends.

**Four, the provision is in the base, in force by its own markers, and was annulled by the Constitutional Court.** Found on 8 September. legis.md leaves the annulled text in place, and after a refresh only the `HCCnn din ...` marker of the amendment that produced that consolidation survives, so an annulment can be invisible in the text held. The register at `_meta/hcc/hcc-register.md` is the control. Of the decisions once listed without an article, most were recovered on 15 September by reading version history and operative parts; the residue is not fixed but moving, because a newly ingested act can bring its own unattributed decisions before anyone reads that act's history.

It moved again on 19 September, in both directions. The register learned a third marker form: some acts write an annulment as `Nota: Art.N ... declarată neconstituțională prin HCCnn ...` rather than the bracket or asterisk forms the parser already knew. The fix is additive, gated on the same identifying pattern as the asterisk form, and checked corpus-wide before being trusted: every existing instance of the new form belongs to the one act it was written for, `COD-325-2022`, so no other act's register entry was rewritten by the change. Ingesting that act then supplied its own residue: four Constitutional Court decisions, of which three carry an attributed article and one, HCC16/2024, is attributed by comparing consolidations rather than by reading the decision's own operative part, a weaker provenance worth knowing before relying on it. Rule, from the register itself: an article listed there is cited only with the decision and the extent of the annulment; an act in the "no article attributed" table is cited with a warning that it carries unverified Constitutional Court decisions.

## The Civil Code, and the general lesson

Until 4 September the Civil Code had no version identity, so it appeared in neither the staleness nor the in-force audit, not because it was clean but because it could not be measured. Measured on that date, the result inverted the expectation: the wiki text applied LP251/2025 in force from 1 April 2026, which the legis.md consolidation doc_id 150561 did not carry, and the frontmatter blocked a refresh that would have rolled the code back five months.

Closed on 6 September: the code was moved onto the legis.md text itself, consolidation doc_id 150498 in force from 1 April 2026, so its identity is now the publisher's and the block is no longer needed. A future consolidation, doc_id 149719 dated 1 January 2027 (LP76/2026), is known and not ingested. One open question remained from the PDF era: arts. 2047 to 2054 were absent under a heading that cited no repealing law, in the PDF and in the legis.md text alike, so only the version history could date that repeal.

Answered 19 September, and worth the wait for what it revealed. The gap was never an unexplained hole: arts. 2047 to 2054 were **Secțiunea a 3-a**, a whole section of Capitolul XXXIV, "Răspunderea pentru dobîndirea, utilizarea sau divulgarea ilegală a secretelor comerciale" (liability for the unlawful acquisition, use or disclosure of trade secrets), added by `L-133-2018` pct. 652 as arts. `1431^1` to `1431^8` in the pre-republication numbering, and repealed as a whole by `L-384-2023` art. 16(3) pct. 2, in force 22.02.2024. The heading above the gap carries the word "abrogată" (repealed); no article inside it ever could, because the repeal was written at section level, not article level. That is a fourth mechanism hiding a repeal, beside the three CLAUDE.md item 3 already named: a section-level repeal annotates the heading, and the articles under it simply are not there to carry a marker of their own. The same pass checked the code's numbering gaps as a whole rather than one at a time, and found six gaps, 14 articles in total, of which only two carry any marker; the other four, including this section, are now named by the article that displaced them (`entities/CC-1107-2002.md`), not left as unexplained holes.

The lesson is worth more than the result. A file that cannot be measured is not a file that is probably wrong. It is a file about which nothing can be said, and the response is to make it measurable rather than to assume in either direction.

The same instinct caught something smaller on 16 September. A corpus-wide fix for article titles broken across two lines was applied to 4,973 headers and, checked against the whole corpus rather than trusted on the file it was written for, surfaced a genuine regression in the Civil Code's own text. The fix is only as good as the check that follows it everywhere, not just where it was aimed.

## Known gaps and unproven findings

Tracked in the vault's `CLAUDE.md` under "Open questions that need Eugen's judgement", and not duplicated here so the two do not drift apart. The same restraint covers a gap's status, not only its existence: where CLAUDE.md records that something was fixed, this document names the item and points to it rather than describing the fix, so the description cannot go stale independently of the fact. The in-force register's blindness to point-structured acts was the case that forced the point, in the 17 September revision; nothing new tested it between 17 and 19 September.

One item belongs here, because it changes how every other gap is read. **Refreshing an act to a newer legis.md consolidation destroys the amendment markers of earlier amendments.** Found on 5 September while verifying `L-234-2016`, which looked defective and was not: art. 24 and the whole of Chapter IV were repealed by LP292/2023, and the markers proving it vanished when the act was refreshed to its 2024 consolidation. So a numbering gap with no marker means "no marker in this consolidation", never "no repeal".

That presumption was tested twice more and held both times. All fifteen unexplained numbering gaps across the anchored acts were checked against the legis.md version history on 5 September, and every one is dated to a repealing law. And on 19 September the same presumption resolved the Civil Code's own oldest gap, arts. 2047 to 2054, above, adding a fourth mechanism to the three already known. The consequence for answers is unchanged: an article absent from a file is presumed repealed, and the date comes from the version history or the repealing act itself, never assumed from the file's silence.

A second, narrower presumption should not be trusted the same way. **legis.md's internal document identifier (doc_id) does not track which version is newest.** Confirmed three times now, most recently and most expensively with `COD-325-2022` on 19 September, where the held PDF's doc_id looked plausible for its stated date and the text looked complete, while the version list showed it three consolidations behind. The only safe check is the version list itself; a doc_id comparison alone proves nothing about recency.

What replaces it is narrower, and binds this document rather than the wiki: no restated description of a limitation or a figure that a script or a register already tracks, because a restatement is exactly what goes stale first.
